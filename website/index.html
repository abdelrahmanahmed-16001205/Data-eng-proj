04 How the two datasets were joined

No shared key, so the join is spatial

The housing file lists affordable projects by building. The meter file lists parking meters by location. They share no record ID, and their address fields and dates do not line up: the meter file is a current snapshot with no history.

  1. Index both files onto a grid. Cells are 0.0009° of latitude by 0.0012° of longitude — about 100 metres on a side at New York’s latitude.
  2. Count within a fixed window. Each housing record sums the active meters in the 7 × 7 block of cells centred on it. That puts the window’s edges roughly 300–400 metres away and its corners up to about 500 metres.
  3. Keep the missing values missing. Records without usable coordinates get no proximity value and are excluded from every share, rather than counted as “no meter nearby”.

05 The story

A geographic association, not a cause

The project links housing records and meter locations with a square-grid proximity window because the two datasets share no direct identifier. The results can point to patterns worth a closer look; they cannot show that meters cause housing development, or that housing changes meter availability.

The question

How do affordable housing records and parking meter locations overlap across New York City, and does that overlap differ by borough, completion year, construction type and meter status?

In the notebook’s recorded run, 75.7% of housing records with usable coordinates had at least one active meter in the selected 7 × 7 window, and 77.2% of meter records with usable coordinates had a housing record nearby. The overlap varied by borough and by the size of the proximity window, so these figures describe the chosen method and this data snapshot.

Key findings

  • Meter coverage is uneven across the city: Queens has 81 active meters per 1,000 affordable units and Manhattan 53, against 13 in the Bronx and 40 in Brooklyn — a six-fold spread from a single method.
  • The yearly share of housing records near an active meter rose from 66% in 2014 to 78–85% since 2020, with a dip to 54% in 2016.
  • New construction records sit slightly closer to meters than preservation records: a median of 9 nearby active meters against 7.
  • Community Board BK-05 has the most new affordable units in the data (10,744) but an average of only 0.7 meters nearby; BX-01 has 6,842 units and 11.6 meters.
  • Meters near new affordable housing are slightly more likely to be inactive: 18.4% against 16.4% elsewhere.
  • The scatter views show only weak rank associations: record size against nearby meters rho = +0.17, affordable-unit share rho = −0.20, three-bedroom share rho = −0.18.
  • Records without a meter nearby have a longer median project duration (628 days) than records with one (429 days), but a quarter of the records in that comparison (25.3%) have a zero-day duration because start and completion dates match, so this comparison is fragile.

Limits

The housing file is a current dataset of project and building records; a row is not necessarily a unique physical building. Meter locations and status are a current snapshot, not historical records for each completion date. A meter is not the same as a parking space, and the grid is not walking distance. Missing coordinates cannot be assigned a reliable proximity count, so those records are excluded from proximity shares rather than counted as unmetered.

Why it matters

Planners and housing advocates can use this as an initial screening view to identify neighborhoods for closer study. Before making policy decisions, they would need historical meter data, parking occupancy, transit access, and a more direct measure of access.
